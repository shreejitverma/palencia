## 1. Why this document exists

Every Palencia employee who touches a diamond — buying it, setting it, photographing it, describing it, or selling it — needs to speak about it accurately. Our brand promise is *"Certified diamonds. Handcrafted jewelry. Honest prices."* Honesty starts with **getting the facts right**, then explaining them in plain language.

This is a training reference, not a script. Learn the science here so you can answer any customer question truthfully, translate a grading report on sight, and never oversell a grade or hide a flaw.

> **The one rule:** If you would not say it to a close friend buying their own engagement ring, do not say it to a customer.

---

## 2. The 4Cs at a glance

The **4Cs** — **Cut, Color, Clarity, Carat** — are the universal language for describing a diamond's quality, standardized by the **GIA (Gemological Institute of America)** in the 1940s–50s. Three of them (color, clarity, carat) describe what nature produced; one (cut) describes what a human craftsman did with it.

| C | What it measures | Graded by | Biggest driver of... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cut** | How well the stone's proportions, symmetry, and polish return light | Human craftsmanship | **Beauty / sparkle** |
| **Color** | Absence of body color (D colorless → Z light yellow/brown) | Nature | Whiteness |
| **Clarity** | Size, number, and position of internal/external characteristics | Nature | Purity |
| **Carat** | Weight (0.20 g = 1 carat) | Nature | Size & price |

**Order of impact on beauty:** Cut → Color → Clarity → Carat. Two diamonds of identical carat, color, and clarity can look dramatically different because of cut. This is the single most important idea in this document.

## 3. CUT — the most important C

Cut is the only C determined by human skill, and it has the largest effect on how a diamond actually *looks*. A large, high-color, high-clarity stone with a poor cut will look dull and lifeless. A well-cut stone of more modest specs will out-sparkle it every time.

Cut grade (as graded by GIA on **round brilliants**) reflects how the facets are arranged to manage light. It is a blend of **proportions, symmetry, and polish**.

### 3.1 The three optical effects a great cut delivers

| Effect | Plain-language meaning | What causes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Brilliance** | The white light that returns to your eye | Internal & external reflection of light |
| **Fire** | The flashes of rainbow color | Dispersion — white light split into spectral colors |
| **Scintillation** | The sparkle and pattern of light/dark as the stone moves | Reflections from facets + contrast |

### 3.2 GIA cut-grade scale (round brilliant)

| Grade | What it means | How we position it |
|---|---|---|
| **Excellent (EX)** | Maximum light return; top ~3% of cut quality | Our recommended default; best value in beauty-per-dollar |
| **Very Good (VG)** | Reflects nearly as much light as Excellent | Excellent value; often indistinguishable to the eye |
| **Good (G)** | Reflects most light; some light escapes | Budget-conscious; be candid it will look slightly less lively |
| **Fair (F)** | Noticeable light loss | We generally do not stock for center stones |
| **Poor (P)** | Significant light leakage; dull | We do not sell as center stones |

> **Fancy shapes:** GIA does **not** issue an overall cut grade for fancy shapes (oval, pear, emerald, etc.) — only polish and symmetry. For those, we assess cut quality using proportions and our own visual QC (see §6). Be honest that "cut grade" on the report applies only to rounds.

### 3.3 Key proportions (round brilliant reference)

These are the proportions associated with well-cut rounds. Use them to sanity-check a stone and to explain *why* a cut grade lands where it does.

| Proportion | Well-cut target range | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Table %** | 53–58% | Too large → less fire; too small → less brilliance |
| **Depth %** | 59–62.5% | Too deep → light leaks out the bottom (looks small/dark); too shallow → "fisheye" / glassy |
| **Crown angle** | 34–35° | Drives fire |
| **Pavilion angle** | 40.6–41.0° | Most critical single angle for light return |
| **Girdle** | Thin–Slightly Thick | Too thin chips easily; too thick hides weight |
| **Culet** | None / Very Small | A large culet shows as a "hole" through the table |
| **Polish & Symmetry** | Excellent–Very Good | Surface finish and facet alignment |

> **Practical tip:** A stone that is too deep carries weight *below* the girdle where you can't see it — you pay for carat that doesn't show as size. This is why depth % matters to value, not just beauty.

## 4. COLOR — the absence of color

For white diamonds, "color" grades the **lack** of body color. The less color, the rarer and (all else equal) the more valuable. GIA grades on the **D–Z scale**, viewed face-down against a white reference under controlled lighting.

- **D–Z** covers colorless to light yellow/brown.
- There is no A, B, or C — GIA deliberately started at D to break from older, inconsistent systems.
- Diamonds with **more** color than Z, or with desirable saturated hues, are graded on the separate **Fancy Color** scale (e.g., fancy yellow, pink, blue) — a different market.

### 4.1 D–Z scale and groupings

| Group | Grades | Appearance | How we talk about it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Colorless** | D, E, F | No detectable color; icy white | Premium, top-tier; D is the rarest |
| **Near-colorless** | G, H, I, J | No color visible to most people once set | **The value sweet spot** — face-up white, especially G–H |
| **Faint** | K, L, M | Slight warmth, more visible in larger stones | Warm/vintage look; great value if the customer likes warmth |
| **Very Light** | N–R | Noticeable tint | Rarely stocked |
| **Light** | S–Z | Clearly tinted | Not stocked as white diamonds |

### 4.2 Honest guidance that matters to real buyers

- **Setting metal changes the game.** A warmer stone (I–K) set in **yellow or rose gold** looks beautifully white because the metal masks warmth. In **white gold or platinum**, a higher color grade (G–H+) keeps the look icy. Always ask the metal before recommending a color.
- **Size amplifies color.** Color is easier to see in larger stones. A J may look colorless at 0.5 ct and slightly warm at 2.5 ct.
- **Color is graded face-down, worn face-up.** Once set, most people cannot distinguish adjacent grades (e.g., G vs H) face-up. Do not let a customer overpay for a grade they'll never see.
- **Shape matters.** Step cuts (emerald, asscher) and some fancies show color more; brilliant faceting hides it better.

## 5. CLARITY — nature's fingerprints

Clarity grades the **inclusions** (internal characteristics) and **blemishes** (surface characteristics) formed as the diamond grew. Grading is done under **10x magnification** by a trained grader; the grade reflects **size, number, position, nature, and relief** of these characteristics.

### 5.1 GIA clarity scale

| Grade | Name | Under 10x | To the naked eye |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FL** | Flawless | No inclusions or blemishes | Flawless; extremely rare |
| **IF** | Internally Flawless | No inclusions; minor surface blemishes only | Flawless-looking |
| **VVS1 / VVS2** | Very, Very Slightly Included | Minute; very difficult to see even for a grader | Clean |
| **VS1 / VS2** | Very Slightly Included | Minor; hard (VS1) to somewhat easier (VS2) to see | Clean ("eye-clean") |
| **SI1 / SI2** | Slightly Included | Noticeable under 10x | Often eye-clean (SI1) to sometimes visible (SI2) |
| **I1 / I2 / I3** | Included | Obvious under 10x | Usually visible; may affect durability/brilliance |

### 5.2 The "eye-clean" concept — our value lever

**Eye-clean** means no inclusions are visible to the naked eye at normal viewing distance (about arm's length). Since jewelry is worn, not examined under a loupe, **eye-clean is what matters to most customers.**

| Reality | What it means for the sale |
|---|---|
| Many **VS2** and **SI1** stones are fully eye-clean | Best clarity value — the smart buyer's grade |
| **SI2** is a coin toss | Some are eye-clean bargains; some show a visible inclusion. **Inspect every one.** |
| **I1–I3** typically show inclusions | We generally avoid for center stones; disclose fully if ever sold |

> **Honesty in practice:** We inspect and (per QC) photograph inclusions. We never call a stone "eye-clean" unless we have verified it. If an SI2 has a visible crystal near the table, we say so and reflect it in the price.

### 5.3 Common inclusions & blemishes

| Characteristic | Type | What it is | Concern? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Crystal** | Inclusion | A mineral crystal trapped inside | Depends on size/color/position |
| **Feather** | Inclusion | A small internal fracture | Usually cosmetic; large/surface-reaching ones can affect **durability** |
| **Cloud** | Inclusion | A cluster of tiny pinpoints | Can dull brilliance if dense ("hazy") |
| **Pinpoint** | Inclusion | Tiny dot | Minor |
| **Needle** | Inclusion | Thin, elongated crystal | Minor |
| **Knot** | Inclusion | Crystal reaching the surface | Can affect durability/polish |
| **Cavity** | Inclusion | Opening on the surface | Can trap dirt |
| **Indented natural** | Inclusion | Original rough surface left on the stone | Usually near girdle; cosmetic |
| **Twinning wisp** | Inclusion | Distorted growth lines | Minor |
| **Graining** | Both | Internal or surface growth lines | Usually minor |
| **Naturals / Nick / Scratch / Polish lines** | Blemish | Surface characteristics | Usually cosmetic |

> **Durability watch-list:** Large **feathers** or **knots** that reach the surface, especially near a point or edge (e.g., the tip of a pear or marquise), can be a chip risk. Flag these in sourcing and protect them in the setting.

## 6. CARAT — weight, not size

**Carat (ct)** is a unit of **weight**, not size: **1 carat = 0.20 grams = 100 points.** So a "75-pointer" is 0.75 ct. Two stones of equal carat can look different sizes depending on cut and shape.

### 6.1 Weight vs. face-up size ("spread")

What a customer perceives as "size" is the **face-up area** (spread), measured in millimeters. Cut proportions decide how much of a stone's weight shows as spread.

- A **deep-cut** stone hides weight below the girdle → looks smaller than its carat.
- A **shallow** or well-spread stone shows more face-up → looks larger, but can sacrifice light return if pushed too far.
- Elongated shapes (oval, marquise, pear, emerald) generally look **larger** than a round of the same carat.

### 6.2 Approximate face-up diameter — round brilliant

Use as a **guide only**; actual spread depends on the individual stone's proportions.

| Carat | Approx. diameter (mm) |
|---|---|
| 0.25 ct | ~4.1 mm |
| 0.50 ct | ~5.2 mm |
| 0.75 ct | ~5.8 mm |
| 1.00 ct | ~6.5 mm |
| 1.25 ct | ~6.9 mm |
| 1.50 ct | ~7.4 mm |
| 2.00 ct | ~8.2 mm |
| 3.00 ct | ~9.4 mm |

### 6.3 "Magic sizes" and buying just under

Prices jump at **psychologically important weights** — 0.50, 0.75, **1.00**, 1.50, 2.00 ct — because demand clusters there. A stone just **under** a magic size (e.g., **0.90–0.98 ct** vs. a full 1.00 ct) can cost meaningfully less while looking nearly identical face-up.

> **Honest value tip:** Recommending a 0.90 ct instead of a 1.00 ct can save a customer real money for a difference of a few tenths of a millimeter no one will notice. This is exactly the kind of trade-off we surface, not hide.

## 7. Diamond shapes

**Shape** is the outline (round, oval, etc.). **Cut** is the faceting quality within that shape. Only **round brilliant** receives a GIA overall cut grade; for all others we assess quality via proportions and visual QC.

| Shape | Faceting family | Character & notes | Looks larger per ct? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Round Brilliant** | Brilliant (57–58 facets) | Maximum brilliance & fire; the sparkle benchmark; only shape with a cut grade | Baseline |
| **Princess** | Brilliant (square) | Sharp, modern; brilliant sparkle; protect the corners | Slightly |
| **Oval** | Brilliant | Brilliant sparkle, elongating on the finger; watch the **bow-tie** (dark center band) | Yes |
| **Emerald** | Step cut | Long, open "hall-of-mirrors" flashes, not sparkle; **shows color & clarity** — buy higher | Yes |
| **Cushion** | Brilliant | Soft "pillow" square; romantic, vintage; good fire | Neutral |
| **Pear** | Brilliant | Teardrop; elongating; watch the **bow-tie** and protect the point | Yes |
| **Marquise** | Brilliant | Football/eye shape; maximizes spread; protect both points; watch bow-tie | **Most** |
| **Radiant** | Brilliant (step outline) | Brilliant sparkle in a rectangular/cropped-corner shape; hides color well | Yes |
| **Asscher** | Step cut | Square emerald with deep "X" / windmill; Art Deco; shows color & clarity | Neutral |
| **Heart** | Brilliant | Romantic; needs good symmetry (even lobes, defined cleft) | Neutral |

> **Step cuts (emerald, asscher):** Large open facets act like windows — they reveal color and inclusions. Advise **higher color and clarity** (e.g., VS2+ clarity, G+ color) for these. Brilliant cuts hide both better, so you can go lower.

> **Bow-tie:** Elongated brilliants (oval, pear, marquise) can show a dark bow-tie across the center. Every one is different — this is a **must-inspect** item, not something the report tells you.

## 8. Fluorescence

**Fluorescence** is a diamond's tendency to glow (usually blue) under ultraviolet light. GIA rates it **None, Faint, Medium, Strong, Very Strong.** It is a natural, common property — roughly a quarter to a third of diamonds show some.

| Fluorescence | Effect | Guidance |
|---|---|---|
| **None – Faint** | No visible effect in normal light | Neutral; no discount or premium needed |
| **Medium – Strong** | In **lower** color stones (I–M), blue fluorescence can make them look **whiter** — a benefit | Can be a value opportunity; be transparent |
| **Very Strong** | Rarely, can cause a slight hazy/oily/milky look in sunlight | Inspect; disclose; typically priced lower |

> **Honest framing:** Fluorescence is not a flaw. In many stones it's neutral or even helpful, and fluorescent stones often cost less. We never let a myth ("fluorescence is bad") talk a customer out of good value — but we always inspect Strong/Very Strong stones for haziness and disclose what we see.

## 9. Natural vs. lab-grown diamonds

Both are **real diamonds** — identical chemically (pure crystallized carbon), physically, and optically. The difference is **origin**: one formed in the earth over billions of years, the other in a lab over weeks. We sell both and we are **always explicit about which is which.**

### 9.1 How lab-grown diamonds are made

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| **HPHT** (High Pressure High Temperature) | Recreates earth-like pressure/heat around a diamond seed to grow crystal |
| **CVD** (Chemical Vapor Deposition) | A carbon-rich gas is broken down in a chamber; carbon atoms deposit layer-by-layer onto a seed |

### 9.2 Natural vs. lab-grown — the honest comparison

| Dimension | Natural | Lab-grown |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Diamond (formed in earth over ~1–3 billion years) | Diamond (grown in weeks) — same material |
| **Look / hardness** | 10 on Mohs scale | Identical — 10 on Mohs scale |
| **Graded on the 4Cs?** | Yes (GIA, IGI) | Yes (GIA, IGI) — same 4Cs |
| **Price** | Higher | Typically **significantly lower** for equivalent 4Cs |
| **Resale / long-term value** | Historically holds value better | Newer market; generally lower resale; prices have fallen over time |
| **Rarity story** | Finite, naturally formed | Producible on demand |
| **Best for the customer who wants...** | Rarity, tradition, long-term value | Maximum size/quality per dollar, a modern/sustainability-minded story |

> **Not a diamond:** **Moissanite** and **cubic zirconia (CZ)** are diamond *simulants*, not diamonds — different materials with different sparkle and hardness. Never conflate lab-grown diamond (real diamond) with a simulant. That distinction is a matter of honesty and, in many places, law.

### 9.3 Disclosure — non-negotiable

- **Every** lab-grown stone is labeled **lab-grown** (or "laboratory-grown") on the product page, the quote, the invoice, and in conversation — never "synthetic" alone in a way that could confuse, and never omitted.
- Lab-grown grading reports come from the same labs (GIA, IGI) and are marked accordingly; GIA reports for lab-grown are titled as **Laboratory-Grown Diamond** reports and lab-grown stones carry a girdle inscription.
- We follow **FTC Jewelry Guides**: a "diamond" with no qualifier means natural; lab-grown must be clearly and conspicuously disclosed with an accepted term (lab-grown, laboratory-grown, or [manufacturer]-created).

## 10. How to talk about the 4Cs honestly

Our job is to help the customer **allocate their budget to what they'll actually see and value** — not to push the highest grades.

| Principle | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| **Lead with cut** | "If we optimize one thing, make it cut — it's what makes a diamond sparkle." |
| **Recommend eye-clean, not flawless** | "You don't need FL. A VS2 or a well-chosen SI1 looks identical to the eye for much less." |
| **Match color to the metal** | "In yellow gold, an H or I looks perfectly white — no need to pay for D." |
| **Use magic-size trade-offs** | "A 0.95 ct looks the same as a 1.00 ct but saves you meaningfully." |
| **Never oversell a grade** | We quote the grade on the **report**, not a rounded-up or flattering version. |
| **Disclose every material fact** | Visible inclusions, Very Strong fluorescence, lab-grown origin, durability flags — always stated up front. |
| **Let the report speak** | We hand the customer the GIA/IGI report and teach them to read it. Transparency is the sale. |

> **Budget-priority rule of thumb** for a classic white look: **Cut (don't compromise) → Color (near-colorless, matched to metal) → Clarity (eye-clean) → Carat (buy just under a magic size).**
